# Piece: audio (music, radio voices, effects, mix)

The user's brief: **80s epic synth** (Vangelis's Blade Runner, Tangerine Dream, John Carpenter, Mass Effect 1), deadly serious stakes against godlike foes, a gritty gallows-humoured crew
(Ghostbusters against Gozer, the Nostromo), never zany. Music composed as MIDI by the agent; the user will swap in their own MP3s later. Radio voices through Trap Door's TTS pipeline
(voice design only, never cloning). Weighty, physical effects with near and far versions. No critic can judge sound: **the user's ears are the judge**, and nobody has heard any of this yet.
Code: `Game/Assets/Monolith/Scripts/Audio` (assembly `Monolith.Audio`, its `README.md`), `Game/Audio/` (the wiring and the self-test), the offline tools in `tools/audio` (its `README.md`),
the score and the voices in `audio_src/`, the built sound in `Game/Assets/StreamingAssets/Audio` (20.5 MB), sources and licences in `audio_src/AUDIO_SOURCES.md`.

## Round 1 (builder, 2026-10-24)

### The method, and why
- **One software mixer, Unity as the sound card.** The game's own mixer (`Dsp/Mixer.cs`: voices, five buses, ducking, a music deck, tone generators, a lookahead limiter) is filled from Unity's `OnAudioFilterRead`.
  Nothing else in the game uses an AudioSource. Because it is plain C# it renders the same in the game, headless in the checks, and **offline in step with a capture run** (800 samples a tick, recorded), which is how the clips with
  sound were made and why every check can run without a sound card. It replaces Unity's mixer and 3D sound, which could not be tested headless, ducked from a sidechain or recorded deterministically. Cost: about 1.4% of a core for the
  music, +2.7% for 40 voices on .NET 9; the player's audio thread (Mono) runs at 3% in the quiet escort opening and 15% to 17% (max 20%) in the dogfight's stress moment with all 64 voices busy.
- **Music is rendered offline from MIDI**, not played by a real-time synth and not made in Python: a C# synth in `tools/audio/Synth` (the DSP is shared with the game, and the Windows Python here has no numpy). Offline buys oversampling-free
  quality at any cost (long reverbs, a master chain), a loop rendered two passes round with the second kept (reverb tails wrap, the seam is exact), loudness set by measurement, and the result is a plain file, so the user's MP3 is the same kind of thing.
  Composition is Python writing standard MIDI (`audio_src/compose`, no packages), one track per instrument named for the synth patch, with expression, filter-sweep and vowel controllers: `audio_src/midi/*.mid` opens in any DAW.
- **The palette** (nothing sampled, nothing General MIDI): Juno-style pad (saw plus PWM pulse plus sub through a 4-pole ladder, chorus II), Oberheim-style warm pad, Solina string ensemble (three-phase chorus), a **CS-80 brass** (two saws and a pulse, the ladder
  filter swells open on a slow envelope and with the expression pedal, a scoop up into the pitch, delayed vibrato, glide: Vangelis), a darker low brass, a Jupiter-style brass stab, a Prophet-style mono lead, a Moog bass, a Carpenter pulse bass, a sine sub with
  saturation, a **Tangerine Dream sequencer** (plucked resonant pulse swept by CC74 over each section) with a dark tape ping-pong echo, an FM bell and electric piano, a **formant choir** (a saw through three vowel filters, five voices, vowel morphing ah to oh),
  a dark drone (low saws, a breathing filter), analog sweeps, and a drum machine in the LinnDrum and Simmons manner (kick, gated snare, clap, electronic toms, metallic hats, crash, taiko, cinematic boom) feeding a **gated reverb** (a bright dense hall, gated for 190 ms),
  with a plate and a hall as the main sends. Each patch is calibrated to the same loudness so 0 dB means the same on every track, and tracks are then fitted to role targets from measured stem loudness (`mixfit.py`).

### The score (all in D minor, 112 BPM, 4/4)
The four loops share **one 32-bar form and one harmony**, so the game changes state by cross-fading aligned loops (a louder state on the next beat with a 0.85 s fade, a quieter one on the next bar over 3.4 s); the mixer keeps a music clock and starts the new
loop at the position the old one has reached. Form: A bars 1-8 (i, VI, iv, V: the lament), B 9-16 (the relative major lifts), C 17-24 (D minor against E flat, the Phrygian second: the dark turn), D 25-32 (the climb back, ending on V so it falls into D minor again). Two themes:
the **call** (A, D, F, then E held: the diaspora's) on brass and choir, and the **lament** (a falling line, A G F E D: what the dead hear). Earth has no theme, only a semitone (D against E flat) and a low horn.

### The voices (`audio_src/voice`: `cast.json`, `collect_lines.py`, `gen_voice.py`, `run_voice.sh`)
Trap Door's Breeze-TTS-2 pipeline, copied and slimmed (Trap Door itself untouched; the WSL install under `~/breeze` is only read). **Voices are designed from text** (age, accent, delivery), seed 42, cfg 4; three seeds tried per line and the best kept
(rejecting silent, clipped or runaway takes; a CUDA fault restarts the resumable run). **156 clips, 486 s**, for 140 combinations of line and speaker, from both mission files and `Resources/AI/radio.json` (every wingman acknowledgement; a line a wingman may say is recorded in each wingman voice).
**Cast and accents (no Scottish anywhere, at the user's request):** Small Mercy's comms officer (a South African woman), Tamsin's captain (an American woman, neutral, not regional: the user found the Irish accent out of place in a sci-fi setting, and she was re-voiced on 2026-10-25), Sable 2 (an Australian man), Sable 3 (a Londoner), Sable 1 (a Northern Englishman, Manchester: the player's own, for lines no wingman is left to say),
Lantern (a deep Texan), **Wick, an alien ally**, and **Earth** in two timbres (Warden's, and the Lector's, an ancient hollow priest-machine).

**Intensity: the crew are frantic under fire** (the user, after hearing the first pass: it "sounds very weirdly calm" in a battle). Every line is tagged **calm, tense or frantic** from its mission context (`collect_lines.py`, `INTENSITY`, the reason beside each: the event that fires it,
the music state at that moment in the auto-play, and what is said: bad news is frantic whatever the music does; the opening drift, the briefing and the end are calm; Earth is always calm). 36 calm, 50 tense, 70 frantic. The wingmen's acknowledgements and situation calls are made in **both** tense and frantic and chosen
at run time by the crew's mood, which `RadioDirector` takes from the music state (ambient calm, battle 1 tense, battles 2 and 3 frantic; the nearest available): in a big fight all 24 acknowledgement lines resolve to the frantic take. How a line gets its delivery, in order:
1. *The model is asked for it.* Tense and frantic lines use the voice's identity (who, accent, timbre) plus a delivery ("panicked and shouting over the roar of battle: very fast, loud, breathless and clipped, the voice straining and cracking ... yet still with a bitter flash of gallows humour, like a marine in a firefight"), not the calm description, whose own words ("calm", "unhurried", "never shouting") fight it;
   and the text is punctuated as a shouter would write it (frantic: every full stop becomes an exclamation mark, commas become dashes). The measured result, raw from the model (calm, tense, frantic): Sable 2 median pitch 121, 185, 205 Hz and 2.0, 3.5, 3.8 words a second, brightness (spectral centroid) 2010, 2283, 2516 Hz; Sable 3 244, 270, 361 Hz, centroid 1937, 2644, 2845 Hz.
2. *It is directed* (`tools/audio/Tools/VoiceActing.cs`): pauses inside the line squeezed (to 0.20 s tense, 0.11 s frantic), the line time-stretched to the pace of someone under fire (3.5 and 4.4 words a second; ffmpeg's rubberband, formants kept so it is the same person, only quicker), and breaths put in: a sharp inhale before it (every frantic line, half the tense ones),
   a gasp in the longest gap, a ragged breath out after a shout.
3. *The radio works harder* (`Dsp/Radio.cs`, `RadioIntensity`): more drive and a hard clip on the shouting (a little clipping: the frantic lines' loudness-to-peak ratio is 7.8 dB against 15.8 dB for calm ones), a presence peak, a louder noise bed with more flutter, a squelch that snaps in 50 ms and out in 110 ms (calm: 160 and 280), and brief drop-outs with a burst of static
   (up to three on a long frantic line). The alien gets a faster buzz, more drive and more clicks. Earth ignores intensity.
4. *It is louder*: calm -23, tense -21.5, frantic -19.5 LUFS (Earth -21).
The radio itself (`Dsp/Radio.cs`, shared with the game so the player's recordings go through it too, at the intensity of the clip they replace): crews are band-limited (320 Hz to 3.3 kHz at calm), levelled, saturated, with a squelch click and burst in and out and a noise bed; **Earth** is the voice doubled an octave and a fourth down and a fifth up, a metallic comb, a slow amplitude flutter, a cold EQ, preceded by a second of power-up
(a sub swell, shimmer) and followed by a 4.8 s hall; **the alien** is a pitch-shifted double with a ring-modulated buzz, a wandering resonance, a purr and chitters under it, and bubbling chirps for its squelch.
The index (`vo/index.json`, format 2) maps the line's text (upper case, spaces collapsed) to the clips per voice and intensity; at run time the speaker's voice first (the intensity nearest the mood, the more urgent when equally near), then any voice's clip of the same words, then a squelch and a logged warning (`[MN][WARN] VOICE no clip for ...`). The player's own recording of the same name replaces a clip
(`..._tense` and `..._frantic` are separate files). Checked by numbers only (pitch, pace, brightness and loudness per voice and intensity: `check_voices.py`; durations; no clipped or runaway takes): whether it sounds panicked, and whether the accents came out as described, is for the user's ears.
**To hear the difference without playing:** `tools/audio/make_voice_reel.py` makes `captures/audio/r1/clips/frantic_reel.mp3` (52 s): six lines, each as the old calm read, the tense one where there is one, and the frantic one.

### The effects (`tools/audio/Sfx`, all synthesised: oscillators, noise, modal and FM synthesis, comb filters, the shared reverbs; **no recording, no library, no CC0 placeholder**)
58 cues, 110 files (variants and distant versions), peak-normalised then given a level in `sfx.json`, with a minimum distance, a near-to-far crossfade range, an arrival delay for far sounds, a priority and a duck. Guns: the player's fast gun (snap, falling saw zap, pulse body, thump; 3 variants), Earth's gun (ring-modulated, colder),
the heavy gun (a slow wallop with a sub boom and a ringing plate), a capital turret, flak and its burst, missiles and torpedoes (a release clunk, ignition, a hiss that climbs) and their passing hiss as a loop with Doppler, decoys and their spoof, the near miss (a ripping Doppler hiss), beams (charge 2.3 s, a crack, a roaring loop of beating saws, the bite, the fall).
Impacts: shield (a bubble, a sizzle, a ring) and hull (a bar's inharmonic ring, a crack, a thud, shards), each in an on-you version with a cabin boom and rattle, a heavy one, shield-down, a subsystem blast, a collision. Explosions in three sizes, a capital ship's break-up (tearing and sixteen clangs), its shockwave (a sub swell you feel). Cockpit: the engine hum (two detuned saws beating, a sub, air; **pitch follows speed**),
the afterburner roar and its ignition, cabin air, a hull alarm (two tones), four hull creaks (more often and deeper under heavier hits), a system failure, a scan sweeping the hull. The jump flash in and out (small and big), a **deep horn when an Earth ship arrives** (low brass chord, a hard bite then dread, a 5.5 s hall). Interface: click, select, target blip, lock chimes, power, radio squelches.
**The lock tone and the missile warning are generated live** (gated sequencer pings whose rate and pitch follow the lock and the missile's ETA), in the mixer, so they duck and limit with everything else; the HUD's stand-in tones (`CombatAudio`) are silenced through `AudioLink.Owned`.
Juice hookup: `AudioRig` gives the juice an `IJuiceAudio` (`JuiceAudioAdapter`) through `JuiceAudioHooks.Factory` (a 5-line hook in `JuiceDirector.CreateAudio`); four cues were added to the enum (capital turret, torpedo, jump in, jump out) and the weapon table and Jumps use them.

### The mix
Buses: music, effects (normal, big, interface), voice. Player volumes (master 0.85, music 0.75, effects 0.9, voice 1.0 by default; a slider position squared) in `audio.json`, keys F2 to F8, - and =. **The voice ducks music by 9 dB and effects by 4.5 dB** (25 ms in, 600 ms out, measured: -9.0 and -4.5);
**a big moment ducks the rest**: each big cue has an amount and a time (a capital ship's death 55% for 3.5 s, a beam 30%, an arrival 35%; measured in the self-test: the music -15.9 dB under a capital death with the voice also speaking, back within a few seconds); stings dip the music 3.5 dB;
pausing mutes the engine and drops the effects 8 dB. Slow motion lowers the pitch of effects. A lookahead limiter at -1 dBFS (verified exactly against a brute-force reference) ends the master: **zero clipped samples** in every check and run.
**Levels by loudness, not by ear:** loops -26, -24, -22, -20 LUFS integrated, stings -17.5 to -23, endings -21 to -24, radio about -23 (Earth -21), effects by peak and level; a scripted battle (battle 3, 30 sounds a second, explosions, a capital death, a radio line) measures -25.7 LUFS integrated at the default volumes,
loudest 400 ms -20.5 LUFS, peak -8.6 dBFS, the limiter never engaged; the live dogfight reads short-term -18 to -26 LUFS with peaks to -4 dBFS. Stems were fitted by role (lead brass 0, drums -1, bass -1.5 to -5, pads -3 to -6, choir -3, bells -7) in K-weighted stem loudness, with the drone and sub held lower so the bands stay balanced
(octave-band profile of the finished battle loops: 63 Hz the loudest, 125 to 1 kHz 2 to 9 dB under it, 4 kHz about -17, 8 kHz about -21).
A busy battle can ask for fifty sounds in a tenth of a second: each cue may start only so often (a gun stays a rattle), a sound too quiet to hear is never started, the mixer skips voices below -90 dB, and 64 voices are mixed with the least important stolen first.

## Round 3 (builder, 2026-10-24): dynamic music, the user's fight theme, the dialogue round trip, Tamsin American

**Dynamic music.** The score has two layers. The base is as before (ambient, battle 1 to 3, endings, stings). Over it, **themes**: a mission's `theme` action (`{"do":"theme","name":"lector"}`, `"on":false` to end, optional `fadeIn`, `fadeOut`, `priority`, `volumeDb`, `mode` duck|replace, `duckDb`) fades a theme track in on the next bar,
ducks (-9 dB) or replaces the base under it, arbitrates by priority when two are on, and fades out on cue; a victory, failure or death ends every theme. Three themes were composed (`audio_src/compose/themes.py`, MIDI in `audio_src/midi/theme_*.mid`): `lector` (choir over a dark pad: Earth's capital ship), `destroyer` (the Warden's beams), `outpost`
(for the Oort mission). Added to the missions: the Lector's in The Crossing and Dead Water (on at its arrival, off when it is destroyed or hit), the destroyer's in the dogfight (on at the first beam, off when the beams are down or the Warden dies). `Mission/README.md` documents the action for the Oort builder.
**`music.json` beside the game** maps every slot (base states, stings, endings, `fight`, themes, each optionally per mission: `"escort/lector"`) to the player's own MP3/WAV/OGG with volume, fadeIn, fadeOut, loop, `bpm` (a track with a tempo changes on its beat/bar, otherwise a time-based cross-fade), loop points, priority and duck; read again every

## Round 4 (builder, 2026-10-24): the sound's share of the start (12.1 s to 7.0 s)

The loading-screen builder found sound decode to be the slowest step of a start (5.7 s of library load with the user's `fight.mp3` in; launch to flyable 12.0 to 12.2 s on this machine against 7.0 s with no audio at all). Measured first (`-mnAudioLoadProbe`, `tools/audio/startup_measure.ps1`), changed second.
- **Where the time went** (one file at a time, 349 files, 6.3 s): Unity's decoder 2.4 s, **inside `DownloadHandlerAudioClip.GetContent`, synchronously on the main thread** (so it is serial, and nothing else can happen meanwhile: the ships took 3.4 s to load instead of 0.4 s because they waited behind it), and then **3.65 s of the main thread** to turn the decoded floats into the mixer's 16-bit clips
  (`Clip.FromInterleaved`: a nested loop with `MathF.Round` and a property read per sample, 30 ns a sample on Mono; fight.mp3 alone 0.61 s, the 16 music loops 1.7 s, the 174 voices 0.86 s, the 148 effects 0.43 s). The copy out of Unity (`GetData`) is cheap (0.1 s in all). The voices were a quarter of the old load (174 clips, 604 s of sound, 1.6 s).
- **The conversion left the main thread** (`Clip.Quantize`, a tight loop; `AudioFileLoader`): the sound is copied out of Unity in pieces of 131072 frames (a few milliseconds of main thread for the whole four-minute MP3, time-sliced to 2 ms a frame), and the thread pool quantises each piece into the clip while Unity decodes the next file; a WAV is read and converted on a worker too.
  A check loads battle2.ogg in 25 pieces and compares it, sample for sample, with one whole copy (identical). The library: **5.7 s to 2.0 s**, flyable **12.1 s to 8.4 s**.
- **The decoded sound is kept** (`PcmCache`): since Unity's decode cannot leave the main thread, a file that has been decoded once is written as plain 16-bit PCM (`Audio\.pcm\<path>.mnpcm`, in the build's Audio folder for ours and the player's own Audio folder for theirs: any MP3, WAV or OGG they drop in or name in music.json, and the user's `fight.mp3`, which stays the shipped
  source; the original is never touched) and read back by worker threads on every later start, valid while the source's length and modification time are unchanged (a changed, truncated or damaged cache is ignored and made again; a folder that cannot be written costs nothing; the Editor does not use it, so the Unity project never grows files; `-mnNoAudioCache` turns it off). **The library: 0.13 to 0.18 s;
  launch to flyable 6.8 to 7.0 s** (the no-audio floor is 7.0 s). `tools/audio/make_playtest.ps1` builds the cache fresh in the playtest folder (about 240 MB, 339 files, 5 s) so the first start there is as quick as the rest; a start with no cache makes it itself (flyable at 8.6 s, once).
- **Tried and dropped: lazy loading.** Loading the ambient bed, stings and endings first and the battle loops, themes and fight theme after (the conductor waiting for a track on its way) measured no better (flyable 8.05 to 8.45 s against 8.4 to 8.5 s), because the decoder is the main thread: deferring a track only moves its 60 to 300 ms stall into flight. Starting the sound after the assets, or the late music after the world,
  made it worse (8.4 to 9.4 s; asking Unity for 342 files at once costs 1.2 s of main thread to issue them). With the cache every file is a worker's read, so there is nothing left worth deferring, and nothing is decoded during play: no hitch when the fight theme fades in (80 s of flight with the AI pilot through the Matins wave and the theme's entry at 58.4 s:
  worst frame 20.9 ms, the same 20.9 ms with `-mnNoAudio`, none over 33 ms; the audio thread's load peaked at 6%).
- **The loading bar's weights.** The sound step ended when the load got round to asking whether it was done (1.6 s), not when it was; it ends when the library is ready now (130 to 137 ms), and the shipped default weight for it is 150 (was 4850), so the bar no longer jumps 45% in the first 0.2 s on a machine with no `loading_stats.json`.
  Measured on this machine (three runs each, `captures/audio/r4/`): launch to the screen 3.5 s; assets loaded 4.3 s (was 9.5 s); world built 5.2 s (was 10.4 s); flyable **6.8 to 7.0 s (was 12.0 to 12.2 s)**; a first start with no cache 8.6 s.
- **Checks:** audio self-test 150 (new: the pieces add up to the whole; the cache is made, read back identically, ignored when the source changes, repaired when cut short or rubbish, harmless where it cannot be written; the suite itself runs in 37 s instead of 70), mission 76 of 76, loading 55 of 55, combat, AI, juice, mixer and flight as before.
- **Least sure of:** 240 MB more in each playtest folder (and on disk in a build once run); the cache assumes a file with the same length and modification time is the same sound (a file replaced by one of the same size with its date kept would play the old one: delete `.pcm`); Unity's own decoder still stalls the main thread once per new or changed file of the player's
  (a four-minute MP3 is 0.3 s, behind the loading screen at the first start after a change, but 0.3 s in flight if the file is dropped in mid-play); the other playtest scripts (`tools/loading`, `tools/hud`) copy the build as it is, so their folders carry the cache only if the build has been run since the last sound change.

### How to run
- The playtest: `Playtest\Monolith_2026-10-24_audio\Play.bat <mission>` (`escort` or `dogfight`; `window`; a difficulty), with `SOUND.txt` and `AUDIO_LIST.txt`. The log it leaves (`Monolith_audio_log.txt`) lists every music change, every radio line with its clip, every line without one, and the audio thread's load.
- Remake the sound: `tools/audio/README.md` (music from MIDI about 8 minutes, effects seconds, voices 5 to 25 s a line on the GPU, resumable).
- Checks: `Monolith.exe -batchmode -nographics -mnAudioSelfTest -mnOut dir`; `tools\audio` `mixertest`, `sheet`, `analyse`.

### Least sure of
- **Everything artistic, because nobody has listened.** The synth and mix follow the real machines and the numbers say it is balanced and clean, but whether the CS-80 brass sounds like Vangelis, whether the choir is a choir, whether the gated snare is gated enough, whether the Earth voice is vast and still intelligible, whether the accents came out as described (Breeze follows text descriptions loosely), whether the horn is a Reaper's, whether the guns are meaty: the user's ears decide. Every layer is a patch parameter or a line in `music.json` and `SfxLib.cs`, so changes are quick.
- Loop fatigue: 68 s loops over a 10 to 15 minute mission, with only four states. The first fix would be a second variant of each loop in another key area or with different rhythmic material; the mixer already takes any number of aligned loops.
- The battle loops are dense and heavily compressed (LRA 1.5 to 2.4 LU): drive without a lot of dynamics. The ambient bed is quiet by design (-26) and may need a lift on a small speaker.
- **Whether the frantic voices sound panicked rather than merely fast and distorted.** The model was asked for a shouting, breathless, cracking read and the numbers say it did something (higher, faster, brighter), and the direction and the radio add pace, breath and grit, but a synthetic voice shouting is the hardest thing here to get right: it may sound like a person reading quickly through a bad radio. If it does, the next steps are
  more pitch and effort on the stressed syllables (per-word emphasis), a second model take per line chosen for roughness, or the user's own shouted recordings dropped in (the radio gives them the same grit). Too much clipping on the frantic lines is one number in `CrewSet` (`Dsp/Radio.cs`).
- Voices: 156 clips by a text-to-speech model that drawls and sometimes rushes; a handful of takes were the best of three seeds, not perfect ones; the accents are only as good as the model's reading of a description (Breeze follows them loosely; a "South African" or "Northern English" voice may be a vague approximation). Wick's alien texture is a processing recipe; it may sound silly.
- The mission's own music rules (hostile counts, `DropAfter`) decide when the intensity changes; the escort mission's first sting and `battle1` fire at 58 s, and Lector's horn arrives together with the battle 3 change at 113 s: the audio follows, it does not choose.
- Trigger rumble and the cabin (breathing, the go-dark moment) are not sounded beyond the scan sweep and dark-cabin hum: the cabin does not exist yet.
- Unity decodes OGG and MP3 for us at start (3.5 s for 302 files; the music is held in memory as 16-bit PCM, about 130 MB); in the player the first radio line can arrive before the sound has loaded (it waits, it is not lost).

### Gotchas found
- `UnityWebRequestMultimedia` needs the `com.unity.modules.unitywebrequest` and `unitywebrequestaudio` packages (added to `Packages/manifest.json`); the first compile after that change reports the type missing, the second is clean.
- Unity refuses an `OnAudioFilterRead` component on an object that also has an AudioListener: the listener has its own object.
- A cue the juice plays with `Play` that is a loop in the catalog (`beam_impact_loop`) must be a one-shot there, or it never ends.
- `ffmpeg`'s Ogg muxer picks a random stream serial, so every encode differs; `-fflags +bitexact` makes regenerating unchanged sound change no bytes in git.
- Offline mode with a recorder grows by 6.4 KB a tick: only a capture asked to record keeps its audio, and headless runs (soaks) have no audio system at all.


## Note from the mission builder (2026-10-24, with the Oort mission): voice.ps1 and lines pending approval

- **`tools/audio/voice.ps1` could not find the WSL path of the repository.** `wsl wslpath -a C:\Users\...` goes through the WSL user's shell,
  which eats the backslashes (`C:Usersx`), so `wslpath` printed nothing and the voice run started in the wrong place. Now it runs
  `wsl -e wslpath -a` with forward slashes (`C:/Users/...`; `-e` runs the program without a shell), throws if the answer is empty, and starts
  the voice script with `wsl -e bash`. Tested (it answers `/mnt/c/Users/chris/<worktree>`); no generation was run.
- **Lines pending the user's approval are never voiced.** The user approves every line before a voice is made (design: the dialogue process).
  A message, inline radio line or call-out with `"pending": true`, or every line of a mission with `"linesPending": true` (the Oort mission),
  is a draft: `audio_src/voice/collect_lines.py` skips it (and prints how many it skipped), so `voice.ps1` makes nothing for it; it plays as
  on-screen radio text with the squelch; `VoiceCoverage` (the mission and audio self-tests) counts it as pending instead of failing, and a
  draft's speaker that is not in `vo/index.json` yet is counted the same way. A message's own `"intensity"` (calm, tense, frantic) is now read
  before the table in `collect_lines.py`. New speakers for the Oort mission are in `cast.json` (Oculus as `earth_lector`; Cantor and Archon as
  `earth_warden`; Patience as `mercy`); `vo/index.json` learns them at the next voice run.
- To voice a line once the user approves it: set `"pending": false` on it (or drop the mission's `linesPending`), run `tools\audio\voice.ps1`,
  rebuild.
